Showing trip prices in different currencies

Research · 10 August 2026 · for Swellyo operator trips (type C)

The one rule every big travel site follows:
  • The price you show and the price you charge are two different things.
  • Show it in the traveler's currency, so they understand it fast.
  • Charge it in one fixed currency, so your money and your books stay clean.
  • Say clearly which one is the real number.

1 · What Swellyo does today

Facts from the code, not from memory.

  • All money is stored in USD. One number, one currency. This is good and should stay.
  • Stripe always charges in USD — price_data[currency] = 'usd' in payments-checkout.
  • Israeli users see ₪ on trip cards and trip price. Everyone else sees $.
  • The ₪ rate is frozen once per trip (budget_fx_rate) and never changes after that. Good idea — the price does not wobble day to day.
  • ₪ browse prices are rounded to the nearest ₪100. Good.
  • The pay sheet shows the exact $ amount plus a ₪ hint next to it.
Two real gaps.
  • An Israeli traveler browses in ₪, pays in $, and then sees a third number on the card statement (their bank's rate + their bank's fee). Three numbers for one trip.
  • FALLBACK_USD_TO_ILS = 3.0. A hardcoded rate standing in for a moving number, with nothing to signal when it drifts. Checked against live: USD/ILS is 2.9987 today, so 3.0 is nearly right — the risk is not today's error, it is that this constant will still be trusted in three years. A wrong price is worse than no price, and no user can spot one.

2 · What the big platforms do

Booking.com display-only conversion closest to us today
  • The hotel sets its price in its own local currency. That currency cannot be changed by the hotel.
  • The guest picks a display currency. It only changes what they see.
  • The charge still happens in the hotel's currency.
  • Booking.com says the converted number is only an estimate, at today's rate.
Lesson: converting for display is normal and accepted — as long as you say it is an estimate.
Airbnb display, charge and payout are three separate settings
  • Host picks a listing currency. Host separately picks a payout currency.
  • Guest picks a display currency, and can actually pay in it.
  • If guest currency ≠ listing currency, Airbnb adds a currency fee — and says so.
  • Airbnb also says clearly: your own bank may add its own fee, and that is not ours.
Lesson: name the fee out loud. Do not hide it inside the rate.
GetYourGuide / Viator tours & activities — closest product to ours
  • Both let the traveler pay in their own currency.
  • Comparison sites measure the markup at about 2% over market for GetYourGuide and 3–4% for Viator. (Not official numbers — reported by review sites.)
  • GetYourGuide shows the full price including fees up front. That is the part reviewers praise.
Lesson: a 2–4% FX markup is normal in this industry. Hiding the total is what people complain about.
Apple App Store real local price lists, not conversion
  • You pick one base price. Apple builds a real price for each of 175 storefronts.
  • Prices are rounded to what feels normal there — $9.99 in the US, ¥1,000 in Japan, ₹699 in India.
  • No user ever sees an ugly converted number like ₪3,842.17.
Lesson: rounding is not cosmetic. A clean number reads as a real price; a converted one reads as a machine guess.
Stripe the payment layer we already use has a ready feature

Stripe's Adaptive Pricing does the whole job for us:

  • Keeps our prices in USD. Stripe shows the traveler ₪ / € / £ at checkout.
  • Rate is mid-market, and guaranteed for 24 hours.
  • Fee is 2–4%, paid by the traveler. We pay 0%.
  • The traveler can still choose to pay in USD if they prefer.
  • Refunds go back in the currency they paid, at the original rate. They get back exactly what they paid.
  • Works with Connect (our operator payouts). Israel is on the supported list.
Also unlocks local payment methods that only work in local currency — iDEAL, Bancontact, BLIK, SEPA, PayPal, Klarna.

4 · Rounding and formatting rules

  • Never round the amount you charge. Round only browse prices. We do this right today.
  • Round to the local habit, not to a formula. $1,200 · €1,150 · ₪4,500 · ¥120,000.
  • Some countries like prices ending in 9 (US, Europe). Some like round numbers (Japan). It is cultural, not universal.
  • Use "≈" or "about" on every converted number. Never on the real one.
  • "$" is ambiguous. US$, CA$, AU$ are different money. Write the code when more than one currency is on screen.
  • Use Intl.NumberFormat, not hand-built strings. It knows where the symbol goes and how many decimals each currency has (JPY has 0, USD has 2).
  • Never show two currencies at the same weight. One is the price. The other is a hint, smaller and grey.
Bad  ₪4,217.44  ·  looks like a machine did it
Bad  $1,200 / ₪4,200  ·  which one am I paying?
Good  ₪4,200 ≈ US$1,200 · charged in US$

5 · The exchange rate itself

  • Freeze one rate per trip. Already done. It keeps the price stable while people decide.
  • Never re-freeze on edit. Already done — an edit must reuse the trip's own stored rate.
  • Refresh the browse rate every 12–24 hours. Faster than that adds nothing for a trip price.
  • Keep the last good rate. If the API is down, use yesterday's real rate — not a hardcoded guess.
  • The rate you charge at must be guaranteed. Stripe guarantees it for 24h. A rate you invent is a rate you carry the risk on.
  • If we ever carry the FX risk ourselves, add a buffer. The industry norm is 2–4%.

6 · Refunds — the part people forget

  • Refund in the currency they paid, at the original rate. This is the whole rule.
  • If you refund at today's rate, the traveler gets back less than they paid, for no reason they can see.
  • They will not blame the exchange rate. They will blame us.
  • Stripe Adaptive Pricing handles this automatically. That alone is a strong reason to use it.

7 · Rules we have to follow

EU — Package Travel Directive applies to our trips
  • A surf trip with accommodation + activity is a package. These rules apply.
  • You may raise the price for exchange rate changes only if the contract says so.
  • Maximum 8%. Above that the traveler can cancel for free and get everything back.
  • You must tell them at least 20 days before travel, with the reason.
Practical result: if we do not write an FX clause into the trip terms, we cannot pass on a currency swing at all.
UK — DMCCA (drip pricing)
  • The total price, with all required fees, must be shown up front.
  • An FX fee added later at checkout is exactly the pattern they are fining.
  • First fine came April 2026 — £4.2m. Travel is named as a focus area.
Israel check with a lawyer
  • Israeli consumer law expects prices to Israeli consumers in shekels.
  • The clear cases are physical shops, but a 2026 draft bill would apply Israeli consumer law to any foreign business that markets to Israelis in Hebrew or in shekels.
  • We already show ₪ to Israelis, so we are on the right side. Just do not go back to $-only for them.
Never do a DCC-style surprise. DCC is when a merchant converts for you without asking, at a bad rate. Markups of 5–18% have been found. Card networks now ban the silent version. Always let the traveler see both options and say what the fee is.

8 · What I would do for type C

  1. Keep USD as the single stored currency. Do not add per-currency amount columns. One truth.
  2. The operator types the price in their own currency. We convert once and store USD + the rate used. Already how it works for Israeli operators — extend it to EUR, GBP, IDR, etc.
  3. Turn on Stripe Adaptive Pricing in Connect settings. The traveler pays in ₪ / € at checkout. We pay 0%. Refunds become exact.
  4. Until that is on: put "≈" on every converted number and write "charged in US$" next to the pay button. One line of copy removes most of the confusion.
  5. Kill the 3.0 fallback for real prices. Store the last good rate in the DB. If we have no rate at all, show $ — never a guessed ₪.
  6. Round browse prices, never charged ones. Already correct — keep it that way.
  7. Put the currency in the operator dashboard exports so the operator's accountant is never guessing.

9 · Checks before turning Adaptive Pricing on

  • Settlement currency. Stripe requires our integration currency (USD) to be a settlement currency of the merchant of record. We use destination charges without on_behalf_of, so the merchant of record is the platform — confirm the platform account settles USD.
  • Checkout, not Elements. Adaptive Pricing does not work with Elements + Payment Intents. We use Checkout Sessions. ✔
  • No custom amounts. Our partial payments compute the number on the server and send a fixed unit_amount, not Stripe's "pay what you want". ✔
  • No manual capture. We do not use it. ✔
  • Israel supported. IL is on Stripe's list. ✔
  • Webhook change needed. The event keeps currency: usd and adds a presentment_details block with what they really paid. Store it, or the support answer to "why does my bill say ₪4,380" is a shrug.

10 · Open questions for you

  • Which currencies do we actually need first — ILS, EUR, USD, GBP? Anything else?
  • Does the operator want to be paid in their own currency, or is USD fine for them?
  • Do we want to take FX risk ourselves (and keep the 2–4%), or hand it to Stripe (and keep 0%)? Handing it over is the safer start.
  • Do the trip terms have an FX clause? Without one, EU rules say we cannot raise a price for currency reasons at all.
